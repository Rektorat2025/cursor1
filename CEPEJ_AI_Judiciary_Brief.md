# CEPEJ & Artificial Intelligence in the Judiciary

**Research brief and persuasive strategy memo for the CEPEJ Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board (AIAB)**

*Prepared: May 2026 | Drafted from open-source desk research*

---

## Part 1 — Research Brief

A synthesis of what CEPEJ has done, what stakeholders are saying, where the evidence points, and how public sentiment is trending around AI in European justice systems.

### 1.1 What CEPEJ is and what it has built on AI

The **European Commission for the Efficiency of Justice (CEPEJ)** is the Council of Europe's standard-setting body on the efficiency and quality of justice across the 46 member states. Since 2018 it has been the most active *judiciary-specific* AI policy actor in Europe, sitting alongside (and feeding into) the broader Council of Europe **Framework Convention on AI and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law** (CETS 225, 2024) and the EU AI Act.

CEPEJ's AI architecture rests on four interlocking instruments:

| Year | Instrument | Status |
|------|------------|--------|
| 2018 | **European Ethical Charter on the Use of AI in Judicial Systems and Their Environment** – the world's first soft-law standard of its kind | In force |
| 2022 | **Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board (AIAB)** – multi-disciplinary expert body | Operational; renewed mandate for 2026–2027 |
| 2023 | **Resource Centre on Cyberjustice and AI** – public registry of judicial AI systems | Operational; to be upgraded into a full **Observatory on AI in Justice** |
| 2023 | **Assessment Tool** operationalising the Charter (complements HUDERIA) | Adopted |
| 2025 | **1st AIAB Report on the use of AI in the judiciary** | Adopted Feb 2025 |
| 2025 | **Guidelines on the Use of Generative AI for Courts** (CEPEJ(2025)18Final) | Adopted at 45th Plenary, Strasbourg, 4–5 Dec 2025 |
| 2026 | **WG-CYBERJUST** new biennial mandate; Plenary in Šibenik, 18–19 June 2026 | Ongoing |

### 1.2 The five Charter principles (the doctrinal anchor)

1. **Respect for fundamental rights** – AI design and deployment must be compatible with ECHR-protected rights (fair trial, presumption of innocence, privacy).
2. **Non-discrimination** – prevent the development *or intensification* of discrimination.
3. **Quality and security** – certified sources, multi-disciplinary modelling, secure environments.
4. **Transparency, impartiality and fairness** – data-processing methods must be accessible, understandable and externally auditable.
5. **"Under user control"** – preclude prescriptive automation; keep users informed and in command.

These five principles are now the lingua franca of European judicial AI governance and are explicitly referenced by both the Council of Europe Framework Convention and several national instruments (e.g. Brazil's CNJ Resolution 615/2025).

### 1.3 Key numbers and stats

- **125 cyberjustice/AI tools** catalogued in the CEPEJ Resource Centre by end of 2024 — the empirical baseline of the **1st AIAB Report (Feb 2025)**.
- **46 % of European Cyberjustice Network respondents** (CEPEJ ECN survey, October 2025) confirmed use of **generative AI in courts**; of those, **74 % use off-the-shelf tools** (Copilot, ChatGPT, DeepL) and **52 % use custom tools** (multi-select).
- **No respondent reported using GenAI for quantitative legal-outcome prediction** — strong empirical pushback against "robot judge" narratives.
- **Future Ready Lawyer 2024** (712 legal professionals, US + 9 EU): **68 %** of law firms and **76 %** of corporate legal departments use GenAI weekly; **35 %** of in-house and **33 %** of firms use it daily. Netherlands leads daily use in Europe at **42 %**, Germany at **38 %**. Primary use cases: document automation (67 %), time management (66 %), strategic planning (62 %). **58 %** of firms and **73 %** of legal departments plan to increase AI investment.
- **KPMG / University of Melbourne global AI trust study 2025** (n ≈ 48 000, 47 countries): only **46 %** of people globally are willing to trust AI; **70 %** say AI regulation is needed.
- **EU AI Act citizen-perception study** (CHI 2026): participants across four EU countries judged **all 48 surveyed AI systems** to pose at least moderate risk and called for **regulation across all tiers**, not only the AI Act's "high-risk" tier.

### 1.4 The flagship judicial AI use cases inventoried by CEPEJ

| Tool | Country | Function | Notable trait |
|------|---------|----------|---------------|
| **OLGA** (OberLandesGerichts-Assistent) | Germany (Stuttgart Higher Regional Court, 2022) | Classifies and triages thousands of diesel-emissions appeals (>100 pages each) | Explicitly "no interference with decision-making"; intelligent research assistant |
| **LEXIQ – Case Law Engine** | Netherlands (Public Prosecution Service, 2017) | Search, analysis, outcome indication for prosecutors | Machine-learning + visualisation; supports — not replaces — prosecutorial judgement |
| **Practical Guide to Justice (GPJ)** | Portugal (Ministry of Justice, 2023) | Conversational AI to guide citizens through judicial services | Trained only on official judicial sources; doesn't generate new legal information |
| **Anonymisation pipeline** | Switzerland (Federal Tribunal) | Automated anonymisation of published decisions | Balances data protection vs. open justice |
| **Transcription tool** | Spain | Court hearing transcription | Workflow productivity |

### 1.5 What the AIAB itself is saying (the risks register)

The 2025 *Reflections of the AIAB on the use of AI in the judiciary* identifies six headline risks:

- **Risk of AI replacing access to a judge.**
- **Risk of vague or unjustified reasoning** in decisions assisted by black-box models.
- **Risk of unfair advantage** to one party, breaking equality of arms.
- **Risk of infringement of fundamental rights** or imbalance in reconciling conflicting rights.
- **Risk of generating non-existent legal provisions** via GenAI ("hallucinations" — directly hitting Charter Principle 3).
- **Risk of reduced judicial responsibility and ownership** ("cognitive surrender" by judges).

The AIAB's doctrinal distinction is sharp:

> *"Symbolic AI systems (expert systems) can be useful for standardised tasks ... Connectionist AI systems (LLM type) can be useful for non-standard, low-risk tasks. For high-risk applications, the use of this type of AI should be carefully considered."*
> — CEPEJ-AIAB(2025)1Rev5, §§23–24

### 1.6 The December 2025 Generative AI Guidelines — what changed

Adopted at the 45th Plenary, the **Guidelines on the Use of Generative AI for Courts** (CEPEJ(2025)18Final) move CEPEJ from principle to practice. Key shifts:

- **Sovereignty over data and infrastructure** is named as a goal: general-purpose tools from large tech firms are "only a first step"; courts should move toward **specialised, locally-deployed LLMs under public control**.
- **Mandatory disclosure** when GenAI is used in preparation of judicial work, with **sanctions** for non-disclosure or over-reliance without verification.
- Courts must be alert to **fabricated evidence, non-existent citations, hidden prompts and undeclared AI assistance** that could break equality of arms.
- **Continuous, standardised training** for judges, prosecutors and staff — including deepfake detection and AI-generated evidence literacy.
- Explicit alignment with **ECHR, Convention 108+, the Framework Convention on AI (CETS 225)**, and the CEPEJ Ethical Charter.

### 1.7 Where CEPEJ is going in 2026–2027

The renewed mandate of the **Working Group on Cyberjustice and AI (WG-CYBERJUST)** sets three pillars:

1. **Monitor** emerging AI systems → transform the Resource Centre into a full **Observatory on AI in Justice**, supporting implementation of the Framework Convention; strengthen the **European Cyberjustice Network (ECN)**; optimise AIAB's role within the Observatory.
2. **Guide** on: process automation in justice (with/without AI), judicial independence via data sovereignty and open-source, legal/practical implications of **AI-generated deepfakes** for electronic evidence, ICT training curricula for judges (presented at June 2026 Plenary).
3. **Build capacity** of justice professionals (training, tech glossary, second AIAB report).

Key milestones: **CEPEJ Plenary, Šibenik, 18–19 June 2026**; **WG-CYBERJUST, Strasbourg, 22–23 October 2026**.

### 1.8 Expert and stakeholder voices

- **Marek Świerczyński** (AIAB member, Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński University, Warsaw) — repeatedly frames CEPEJ's work as setting "ground-breaking standards" and stresses that AI in courts must be evaluated against the full ECHR fair-trial doctrine.
- **Jan Spoenle** (Judge, Stuttgart Higher Regional Court, CEPEJ Pilot Court Network) on OLGA: *"OLGA has no interference with decision-making. It is an intelligent research assistant. It saves time-consuming copying and pasting."*
- **Dr Michael Veale** (UCL Faculty of Laws; UK ICO Technology Advisory Panel) — frequent panel voice arguing CEPEJ's principle-based approach must now be matched by enforceable institutional governance, not just guidance.
- **UNODC / Doha Declaration commentary (2025)**: *"The general consensus by now is that AI should remain under human control, as advocated by the 2018 Ethical Charter ... Judiciaries and courts should institutionalize this control, and that demands new forms of governance."*
- **Consultative Council of European Judges, Opinion 26**: judges must be kept up to date with technology to ensure their effective involvement and concurrence in its adoption.
- **Brazilian CNJ Auxiliary Judge Dorotheo Barbosa Neto** (cited in *Estadão*, 2025): GenAI hallucinates **"in more than 70 % of cases"** when asked to construct legal reasoning before reaching a conclusion — a striking warning shot that has been picked up across Lusophone judicial debate.
- **Critic perspective** (Sipoch / Estadão op-eds, 2025): risk that *"the judge becomes the mouthpiece of artificial intelligence"*, especially where digital literacy and infrastructure are weak.

### 1.9 Recent articles and coverage (selected)

- **BABL AI (2025)** — "Council of Europe Releases First AI Advisory Report on Justice Systems" — frames the report as **rebutting the "robot judge"** narrative and emphasising human oversight.
- **EEEI – Experts Institute (2026)** — "AI and Cyberjustice: the CEPEJ's New European Priorities for 2026–2027" — first detailed public read-out of the WG-CYBERJUST mandate and the Observatory plan.
- **Bingham Centre / BIICL (2025)** — convened a member-state panel on the CEPEJ Charter, the Framework Convention, and the 1st AIAB Report.
- **Universidad de los Andes – Sistemas de Algoritmos Públicos (2026)** — formally catalogues the December 2025 GenAI Guidelines as the *de facto* international standard for courts.
- **Hengeler Mueller (2025)** — Germany-specific read-out on AI in dispute resolution: cautious adoption, no nationwide deployment expected near-term.
- **JuLIA Handbook on Justice (2025)** — distributed across Italian and EU judicial training colleges, references CEPEJ as the spine of European judicial AI ethics.

### 1.10 Public and user sentiment — the trend lines

Aggregating the available evidence yields a consistent five-part picture:

1. **High demand for regulation** — 70 % globally (KPMG 2025); EU citizens want oversight that goes *beyond* the AI Act's high-risk tier (CHI 2026).
2. **Moderate-to-low trust in AI generally** — only 46 % globally are willing to trust AI; trust drops further when the decision-maker is a court.
3. **Strong support for "human-in-control"** — citizens, judges and academic studies converge on requiring meaningful human oversight, especially for legal decisions; concerns about *over-trust* (automation bias) and loss of empathy.
4. **Pragmatic openness to back-office use cases** — anonymisation, transcription, document triage and citizen-facing chatbots draw broad support; outcome prediction and decision automation do not.
5. **Rising anxiety about deepfakes and synthetic evidence** — now a top-of-mind risk for both CEPEJ and national councils (CNJ Brazil Res. 615/2025; CEPEJ GenAI Guidelines 2025).

### 1.11 What this means in one paragraph

CEPEJ has moved decisively from a *principles era* (2018 Charter) to an *operational era* (2023 Assessment Tool, 2025 GenAI Guidelines, 2026 Observatory). The institutional centre of gravity is shifting from doctrine to monitoring, training and tooling. The AIAB sits at the hinge: it is the only standing expert body in Europe with both the technical breadth and the institutional legitimacy to convert the Framework Convention's promise into court-floor practice — but it is competing for narrative space with the EU AI Act, with private legal-tech vendors, and with rising public anxiety about hallucinations, deepfakes and cognitive surrender.

## Part 2 — Persuasive Strategy Brief for the CEPEJ AIAB

*Audience: Members and Secretariat of the CEPEJ Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board, ahead of the 2026 Šibenik Plenary and the second AIAB Report cycle.*

### 2.1 Strategic thesis

> **The AIAB should reposition itself, in the 2026–2027 mandate, from an "expert advisory body" into the operational *trust anchor* for AI in European justice — the body that converts the Council of Europe's normative architecture into evidence, tooling and assurance that judges, citizens and ministries can actually use.**

This thesis is grounded in three convergent realities:

1. **Doctrinal saturation.** Europe now has more *principles* on judicial AI than any other region. The marginal value of yet another set of principles is low.
2. **Empirical scarcity.** Despite 125 listed tools and rising GenAI deployment, *systematic, comparable, longitudinal evidence* on how AI affects judicial outcomes, fairness and efficiency is still missing. Whoever owns that evidence will own the narrative.
3. **Trust deficit.** With only 46 % of citizens willing to trust AI and 70 % demanding regulation, the institutions that visibly *verify* AI in courts will be the institutions that retain public legitimacy.

The AIAB can occupy that verification space — but only if it consciously shifts posture.

### 2.2 The five strategic moves

#### Move 1 — Make the Observatory on AI in Justice the AIAB's flagship deliverable

The transformation of the Resource Centre into a full **Observatory on AI in Justice** is the single highest-leverage opportunity of the new mandate. The AIAB should:

- **Co-brand and co-govern** the Observatory rather than treating it as a Secretariat asset. Public legitimacy follows visible expert ownership.
- Push for the Observatory to publish **annual indicators**: number of systems deployed, risk-tier distribution, share with human-in-the-loop guarantees, share with documented Charter-compliance assessments, deepfake-incident reports submitted by member states.
- Position the Observatory as **the European reference data source** that feeds the Framework Convention implementation reviews — so that every CoE member state has a reason to contribute data.

**Why it persuades:** turns the AIAB from an advisory committee into a *data publisher*. Data publishers shape debates; advisors react to them.

#### Move 2 — Own the "judicial-grade AI" standard

Private legal-tech vendors are racing to claim the "AI for courts" label. The Charter, the Assessment Tool and the GenAI Guidelines already give the AIAB the substantive material to define what *judicial-grade* means. The AIAB should formalise this into a recognisable **CEPEJ Judicial-Grade AI conformity scheme**, covering:

- documented human-oversight architecture;
- traceable data sources and certified training corpora;
- hallucination-rate disclosures, with thresholds;
- equality-of-arms safeguards (no covert prompt asymmetries between parties);
- sovereign-deployment / open-source readiness;
- mandatory disclosure logs when used in judicial drafting.

This is the **only realistic European alternative** to letting the EU AI Act's generic high-risk tier — or vendor self-attestation — become the de facto standard for courts.

**Why it persuades:** gives ministries of justice, court IT directors and procurement teams a *single* CEPEJ benchmark to write into tenders. It converts soft law into procurement law without any new treaty.

#### Move 3 — Lead, don't follow, the deepfake and AI-evidence agenda

The 2025 GenAI Guidelines flagged deepfakes as a priority; the WG-CYBERJUST 2026–2027 mandate names them explicitly. The AIAB should claim ownership of this dossier before national supreme courts or the EU set incompatible standards:

- Publish a **CEPEJ Deepfake & Synthetic Evidence Note** (technical + procedural) jointly with forensic experts.
- Promote a **shared incident-reporting taxonomy** through the European Cyberjustice Network so that synthetic-evidence cases become visible across borders.
- Build a **judicial detection toolkit reference list** within the Observatory.

**Why it persuades:** deepfakes are the issue on which judicial AI risk meets daily public anxiety. A visible CEPEJ-AIAB lead here will define the body's public profile for the rest of the mandate.

#### Move 4 — Institutionalise "judges in the loop" through training, not just text

The June 2026 Plenary will receive the **ICT training curriculum for judges**. The AIAB should treat that curriculum as a *governance instrument*, not a pedagogical formality:

- Embed the **Charter's five principles + the GenAI Guidelines** as the assessment frame for every module.
- Push for **mandatory baseline competence** in: prompt and context engineering, hallucination detection, AI-evidence forensics, data-protection hygiene under Convention 108+.
- Partner with national judicial schools (e.g. ENM France, EJTN, Scuola Superiore della Magistratura, German Richterakademie) for cascade delivery.
- Track adoption through Observatory indicators — closing the loop with Move 1.

**Why it persuades:** answers the most consistent expert consensus across the literature (UNODC, CCJE Opinion 26, KPMG, German position paper) that **judges must be capable users**, not passive recipients, of AI.

#### Move 5 — Speak publicly and in human language

The AIAB's outputs are technically excellent but rarely break out of expert circles. To convert authority into influence:

- Publish each AIAB Report in a **short public-facing companion brief** (≤ 8 pages, plain language, infographics from Observatory data).
- Hold an **annual AIAB public hearing** alongside the December Plenary, with civil-society, bar association and citizen-rights organisations.
- Run a **rolling explainer series** (5–10 minute videos / blog posts) on key risks: hallucinations, deepfakes, equality of arms, automation bias, data sovereignty.
- Pre-brief journalists and academics ahead of major releases — the EEEI, BIICL and BABL coverage of 2025 shows there is appetite when CEPEJ engages.

**Why it persuades:** the trust deficit will not be closed by guidelines alone. Public legitimacy is built by *being seen* to do the work.

### 2.3 Twelve-month operational roadmap (deliverables, not dates)

| # | Deliverable | Owner within AIAB | Tie-in |
|---|-------------|-------------------|--------|
| 1 | Observatory governance charter (co-signed AIAB + WG-CYBERJUST) | Observatory rapporteur | Move 1 |
| 2 | Second AIAB Report — first edition using Observatory v2 data | Report drafting team | Moves 1, 5 |
| 3 | "CEPEJ Judicial-Grade AI" conformity framework (draft for plenary) | Standards sub-group | Move 2 |
| 4 | Deepfake & Synthetic Evidence Note | Joint AIAB + forensic experts | Move 3 |
| 5 | ICT training curriculum endorsement and competence baseline | Training sub-group | Move 4 |
| 6 | Public-facing companion brief + first explainer series episodes | Communications lead (to be designated) | Move 5 |
| 7 | Annual AIAB public hearing at December plenary | AIAB Bureau | Move 5 |

### 2.4 Risks to the strategy and how to mitigate them

| Risk | Mitigation |
|------|------------|
| **Overlap / friction with the EU AI Act and the Committee on AI (CDNET/ex-CAI)** | Frame CEPEJ instruments as the *judicial-sector specification* layer of the Framework Convention and AI Act, not as competitors. Maintain joint sessions with CDNET. |
| **Member-state data scarcity** | Use the European Cyberjustice Network and biennial Evaluation cycle as the data-collection backbone; tie Observatory contributions to existing reporting obligations. |
| **Vendor capture of "judicial-grade" labelling** | Keep the conformity framework anchored in independent expert review; AIAB members must declare interests publicly. |
| **Public perception that CEPEJ enables, rather than restrains, AI in courts** | Lead with the deepfake and equality-of-arms narrative (Move 3) and with visible refusal of full automation in decision-making. |
| **Resource constraints on the Secretariat** | Mobilise AIAB members as named rapporteurs per workstream; leverage observer institutions (EEEI, BIICL, judicial training schools, World Bank Digital Justice WG). |

### 2.5 The one-page elevator argument

- Europe has the *most advanced doctrine* on judicial AI in the world. It does not yet have the *most advanced practice*.
- **125 tools** are deployed, **46 %** of European courts surveyed already use generative AI, **70 %** of citizens demand regulation, **only 46 %** trust AI — and the leading risks (hallucinations, deepfakes, equality of arms, automation bias) are now empirically documented.
- The CEPEJ AIAB is the only body in Europe with the mandate, the cross-disciplinary composition and the institutional cover to translate the **Framework Convention on AI** and the **2025 Generative AI Guidelines** into something that judges, citizens and ministries can actually trust.
- To do that, the AIAB should: **(1)** own the Observatory, **(2)** define "judicial-grade AI", **(3)** lead on deepfakes and AI evidence, **(4)** make judges capable users through training, and **(5)** speak publicly.
- The window is open at the **18–19 June 2026 Šibenik Plenary**. After that, the EU AI Act's high-risk regime and private vendors will increasingly define what AI in European courts looks like — by default rather than by design.
