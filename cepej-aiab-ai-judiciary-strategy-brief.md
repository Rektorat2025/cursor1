# Strategy Brief: Making AI Work for the Judiciary

### A Persuasive Brief for the CEPEJ Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board (AIAB)

> **Bottom line up front:** AI is already inside Europe's courts — at least **125 catalogued systems** and growing fast — but adoption is uneven, public trust is fragile, and the gap between high-level principles and day-to-day practice remains wide. The CEPEJ has the only purpose-built, justice-specific governance stack in the world (Charter → Assessment Tool → Resource Centre → AIAB → Generative AI Guidelines). The strategic opportunity for the AIAB is to convert that authority into **operational, measurable, and trusted adoption** before commercial general-purpose tools fill the vacuum on their own terms.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence in the judiciary has crossed from speculation into routine. The Council of Europe's CEPEJ — through its **Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board (AIAB)**, created in 2022 — now sits at the centre of the European response. The evidence base assembled by the AIAB and the Resource Centre on Cyberjustice and AI tells a consistent story:

- **AI is mainstream but supportive, not autonomous.** As of end-2024, **125 cyberjustice/AI tools** are catalogued, overwhelmingly used as assistance tools. There are **no fully autonomous systems** deciding cases — and the evidence does not support the "robot judge" narrative.
- **The governance scaffolding is the most mature in the world.** The 2018 Ethical Charter (5 principles), the 2023 Assessment Tool (29 verifications), the Resource Centre (2023), the 1st AIAB Report (2025), and the **Guidelines on the Use of Generative AI for Courts** (adopted 4–5 December 2025) form an end-to-end framework.
- **Trust is the binding constraint.** Independent research consistently finds the public trusts human judges more than algorithmic ones — especially in emotionally complex cases — but that **judicial leadership and visible human oversight materially raise trust**.
- **The risk window is open now.** Generative AI use is rising sharply; if courts default to generic commercial chatbots without sovereign, secure, court-tailored tooling, fundamental-rights and data-sovereignty risks will compound faster than safeguards can catch up.

**The ask:** The AIAB should move from *principles and monitoring* to *operationalisation and outcomes* — driving uptake of the Assessment Tool, expanding the Resource Centre into a living evidence base, accelerating sovereign generative-AI deployment models, and publishing trust and adoption metrics annually.

## 2. The Landscape: Data, Stats & Trends

### 2.1 Adoption snapshot (CEPEJ Resource Centre, end-2024)

- **125 systems** catalogued, "aiming at improving judicial efficiency and accessibility, primarily from Europe."
- Distribution **varies markedly by country** — adoption is real but uneven.
- Tools are **mostly internal IT systems** (not public-facing), which the AIAB stresses does *not* exempt them from transparency and accountability requirements.
- Machine learning and natural language processing dominate; **generative AI is the fastest-rising category**.

### 2.2 Where AI is actually being used (by area of application)

> Tools are tagged by primary function; totals exceed 125 because systems can address multiple tasks.

| # | Area of application | Tools |
|---|---------------------|------:|
| 1 | Decision support | **41** |
| 2 | Triaging, allocation & workflow automation | **38** |
| 3 | Document search, review & large-scale discovery | **32** |
| 4 | Anonymisation & pseudonymisation | **19** |
| 5 | Information & assistance services (e.g. chatbots) | **17** |
| 6 | Recording, transcription & translation | **13** |
| 7 | Online dispute resolution (ODR) | **7** |
| 8 | Prediction of litigation outcomes | **3** |

**Read the pattern:** the centre of gravity is **back-office efficiency and access** (workflow, search, anonymisation, transcription), *not* predictive adjudication. Outcome-prediction tools are the smallest category (3) — a signal that practitioners themselves are cautious about the highest-risk use case.

### 2.3 Flagship examples in production

- **OLGA (Germany)** — OberLandesGerichts-Assistent: classifies high-volume "diesel" emissions claims (complaints often exceeding 100 pages) at the Higher Regional Court in Stuttgart; saves cost and time on mass litigation.
- **ANOM (Switzerland)** — neural anonymisation that detects and replaces names/identifiers of non-parties in judgments.
- **Practical Guide to Justice / PGJ (Portugal)** — conversational ML model that helps citizens and businesses navigate available justice services.
- **Speech-to-Text "Textualisation" (Spain)** — neural transcription integrated with courtroom recording, with speaker identification and searchable timelines.
- **LEXIQ (Netherlands)** — supports prosecutors by predicting litigation outcomes.

### 2.4 The regulatory tide

- **Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (CETS 225)** — the first binding international AI treaty, anchored in human rights, democracy and the rule of law.
- **EU AI Act** — classifies the use of AI in the administration of justice as **high-risk**, triggering stricter obligations.
- **Convention 108+** — data-protection backbone for automated processing.
- **HUDERIA** (Committee on AI / CAI) — human-rights/rule-of-law impact assessment methodology the CEPEJ Assessment Tool is designed to complement.

**Trend:** binding regulation is arriving fast. The CEPEJ's comparative advantage is being **justice-specific and operational** where the horizontal instruments are general.

## 3. Expert Position & Notable Voices

### 3.1 The CEPEJ/AIAB doctrine (in its own words)

- **Human control is non-negotiable.** "In the jurisdictional field, human control and supervision must never be lost sight of." AIAB warns explicitly against **automation bias** — "the tendency of human beings to uncritically consider the solution proposed by artificial intelligence as correct."
- **AI assists; it does not decide.** AI systems "must be considered as assistance and support tools, without attributing … a decisive value to them," preserving "the necessary judicial motivation and the indispensable individualisation of sentences."
- **A right to human review.** The right "to challenge or review automated or algorithmic decisions and to request human supervision and intervention should be guaranteed."
- **Right tool for the right task.** Deterministic *symbolic* (expert) systems suit standardised tasks; *connectionist* (LLM-type) systems suit non-standard, **low-risk** tasks — and "for high-risk applications, the use of this type of AI should be carefully considered."

### 3.2 The five principles of the 2018 Ethical Charter (the spine of everything)

1. **Respect for fundamental rights** — design and use compatible with human rights.
2. **Non-discrimination** — prevent creating or intensifying discrimination.
3. **Quality and security** — certified sources, multidisciplinary models, secure environments.
4. **Transparency, impartiality and fairness** — accessible, understandable methods; allow external audits.
5. **Under user control** — no prescriptive approach; users stay informed and in control.

### 3.3 The 2025 Generative AI Guidelines — core commitments

Adopted at the **45th plenary (4–5 December 2025)**, the Guidelines are built on four hard limits and a phased method:

- **Judicial power remains the exclusive responsibility of the courts.**
- **Effective access to a human judge is always guaranteed.**
- **Generative AI outputs are never binding.**
- **Use must be fully transparent.**
- **Subsidiarity:** deploy generative AI only where conventional solutions are ineffective; **prefer customised, secure, publicly-controlled tools** (and where appropriate, local/sovereign LLMs) over generic off-the-shelf products.
- **Phased rollout** with pilots, metrics, training, citation-verification, and the ability to **reverse course**.

### 3.4 The jurisprudential cautionary tale

- **State v. Loomis (Wisconsin, 2016)** — the court permitted use of the proprietary **COMPAS** risk-assessment tool but cautioned it "should not be used to determine whether an offender is incarcerated or to determine the severity of the sentence." It remains the cornerstone precedent that opaque, proprietary scoring **cannot replace judicial discretion** — a real-world validation of the Charter's transparency and user-control principles.

## 4. User Sentiment & Public Trust

Independent empirical research converges on a clear, actionable picture:

- **Humans are trusted more than algorithms.** Across experiments (N ≈ 1,800–3,000), participants perceive **human judges as more trustworthy** and are more willing to bring cases before them than before algorithmic judges.
- **Trust collapses in emotionally complex cases.** Algorithmic trust is "especially low when legal cases involve emotional complexities," but closer to human levels for technically complex or routine matters — mirroring the AIAB's low-risk/high-risk distinction.
- **Task matters.** The public is comfortable with AI doing **financial calculations**, far less so with **bail eligibility, sentence length, or drafting legal findings**.
- **Judicial leadership is the lever.** Public trust in AI rises significantly when **judges themselves visibly trust and oversee** the tools — judicial endorsement and explicit human oversight are the strongest trust multipliers.
- **Bias is the recurring fear.** Open-ended responses repeatedly cite worry that AI will **perpetuate or amplify existing biases**, especially against minorities — directly engaging Charter Principle 2 (non-discrimination).
- **Equity nuance:** some studies find demographic differences in perceived fairness (e.g., Black participants reporting greater trust in AI-augmented decisions than White or Hispanic participants), underscoring that "trust" is not monolithic and must be measured across groups.

**Strategic implication:** trust is not won by better models alone. It is won by **visible human oversight, transparency, demonstrated non-discrimination, and confining AI to tasks the public already accepts** — exactly the levers the CEPEJ framework already names.

## 5. The Strategic Case for the AIAB

### 5.1 Why now

1. **First-mover advantage is perishable.** Generative AI uptake is accelerating; if courts adopt generic commercial chatbots ad hoc, governance becomes retrofitting rather than design.
2. **Binding law has arrived.** With the Framework Convention (CETS 225) and the EU AI Act treating judicial AI as high-risk, member states **need operational compliance support now** — precisely the AIAB's niche.
3. **The evidence base is live and unique.** The Resource Centre is the only public, justice-specific AI registry of its kind. Its value compounds with every entry and every annual report.
4. **Trust is fragile and movable.** The research shows trust is low by default but **responsive to oversight and transparency** — meaning deliberate AIAB action can measurably shift it.

### 5.2 Strengths to build on (CEPEJ's moat)

- A complete, **internally consistent stack**: Charter → Assessment Tool → Resource Centre → AIAB advice → Generative AI Guidelines.
- **Multidisciplinary, member-state-backed legitimacy** and alignment with ECHR / Convention 108+ / HUDERIA.
- Concrete, **replicable national exemplars** (OLGA, ANOM, PGJ, Textualisation, LEXIQ).

### 5.3 Gaps to close

- **Principle-to-practice gap:** adoption of the 29-question Assessment Tool is not yet systematically tracked or required.
- **Uneven diffusion:** benefits concentrate in a few member states; smaller jurisdictions risk being left behind.
- **Trust deficit & measurement gap:** no standing, comparable metric of public or practitioner trust.
- **Procurement risk:** dependence on opaque, proprietary commercial tools cuts against transparency, auditability, and data sovereignty.

## 6. Recommended Strategy for the AIAB

### Pillar 1 — Operationalise (turn principles into defaults)
- Make the **Assessment Tool** the expected gate for any court AI procurement or deployment, with a lightweight self-attestation template mapped to the 5 principles and HUDERIA.
- Publish **plain-language deployment playbooks** per application area (workflow, anonymisation, transcription, decision support), each with do/don't checklists and a worked example from the Resource Centre.

### Pillar 2 — Evidence (make the Resource Centre the authoritative monitor)
- Commit to the **annual AIAB report** as a fixed cadence; expand fields to capture risk classification, human-oversight design, audit status, and outcomes/benefits realised.
- Add a **per-country adoption dashboard** to expose and close diffusion gaps.

### Pillar 3 — Sovereign generative AI (own the infrastructure, not just the rules)
- Operationalise the 2025 Guidelines' preference for **customised, secure, publicly-controlled tools and local/sovereign LLMs** via shared reference architectures and pooled procurement for smaller member states.
- Mandate **citation-verification, pseudonymisation, transparency labelling, and reversibility** as baseline controls for any generative deployment.

### Pillar 4 — Trust (measure it, then move it)
- Launch a recurring **public-and-practitioner trust survey**, reported alongside the annual report, stratified by demographic group and by task type.
- Promote **judicial-leadership programmes** (training + visible oversight) — the single strongest trust multiplier the evidence identifies.
- Default to AI on **publicly-accepted, low-risk tasks first**; require heightened justification and human review for bail, sentencing, and drafting of reasoned decisions.

### Pillar 5 — Capacity & alignment
- Scale **training** for judges and court staff (literacy on hallucination, bias, automation bias, prompt hygiene, confidentiality).
- Keep the AIAB tightly **interoperable with the CAI, CDCJ, the EU AI Act regime, and Convention 108+** so courts face one coherent compliance pathway, not several.

## 8. Risks & Counter-Arguments (and responses)

- **"This slows innovation."** → Phased rollout *enables* innovation by de-risking it; reversibility prevents costly lock-in. The Guidelines explicitly favour pilots over moratoria.
- **"Generic commercial tools are cheaper and faster."** → They externalise data-sovereignty, confidentiality, and auditability risk onto the court. Pooled sovereign procurement narrows the cost gap while keeping control public.
- **"The public will accept AI eventually."** → Evidence says trust is *not* automatic and is lowest exactly where stakes are highest; passive acceptance is not a strategy — visible oversight and measurement are.
- **"Principles are enough."** → Principles without operational uptake leave a vacuum that commercial defaults will fill. The Assessment Tool and metrics convert principles into practice.

---

## 9. Conclusion

The CEPEJ AIAB holds a position no other body in the world occupies: a **justice-specific, rights-anchored, member-state-legitimised** governance stack arriving exactly as binding AI law lands and as generative AI floods into courts. The data shows AI in European justice is **real, supportive, and uneven**; the expert doctrine is **clear and human-centred**; and public trust is **fragile but movable** through oversight and transparency.

The decisive move is to shift from *describing* good practice to *defaulting* to it — embedding the Assessment Tool, expanding the evidence base, building sovereign generative-AI infrastructure, and measuring trust. Do this, and the AIAB does more than keep "robot judges" out of the courtroom: it makes AI a trusted instrument **in the service of the general interest**, with the human judge — and human rights — firmly in control.
