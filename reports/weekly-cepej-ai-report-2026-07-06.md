## 1. Executive summary

No clearly new official CEPEJ AI instrument dated 2026-07-01 through 2026-07-06 was found in accessible public search results. The strongest current-week signal was social/professional circulation around a 2026-06-29 Cyber Jurisprudence International Initiative session, "Ethical use of AI in Judiciary: Focus on CEPEJ - CoE Documents", promoted in a public LinkedIn result by Dr Arben Murtezic and reposted by A4ID.

The most prominent non-social references remain the late-June public coverage of the 46th CEPEJ plenary meeting in Sibenik, Croatia, and institutional references to the CEPEJ Working Group on Cyberjustice and Artificial Intelligence. These references frame CEPEJ's AI work around implementation rather than new principle-setting: a proposed Observatory on AI in Justice, a draft technology curriculum for judges, a second AIAB report, and guidance on automation, data sovereignty, open-source software, and AI-generated deepfakes.

Public legal and policy references continue to cite the 2018 CEPEJ European Ethical Charter as a baseline instrument and the 2025 CEPEJ Guidelines on the use of generative artificial intelligence for courts as the current operational framework. Repeated quotations emphasize human judicial authority, non-binding AI outputs, transparency, traceability, data protection, and fundamental-rights safeguards.

## 7. Trends and interpretation

1. **From charter principles to implementation controls.** Public references increasingly pair the 2018 Ethical Charter with concrete operational measures: assessment tools, GenAI court guidelines, auditability, data control, source validation, and risk/impact assessment.

2. **Human judicial authority is the stable core message.** Across official CEPEJ sources, EU policy references, media coverage, and social snippets, the repeated theme is that AI can support judicial work but must not replace judges or create binding automated outcomes.

3. **Observatory and monitoring language is becoming central.** The proposed Observatory on AI in Justice, the AIAB, the European Cyberjustice Network, and the Resource Centre are repeatedly cited as the infrastructure for tracking AI systems and emerging risks.

4. **Capacity building is moving up the agenda.** The model technology curriculum for judges appeared in plenary coverage and in the 2026-2027 work programme. This suggests CEPEJ is treating AI literacy as a governance safeguard, not merely a training add-on.

5. **Deepfakes, data sovereignty, and open-source software are emerging risk areas.** The 2026-2027 priorities extend beyond generative text tools into electronic evidence, independence-preserving infrastructure, and public control of judicial data.

6. **This week's visibility is professional rather than mass-media driven.** No new mainstream media item was found in the monitoring window. The CEPEJ AI discourse is currently moving through Council of Europe pages, observer organizations, legal-policy databases, academic/event pages, and LinkedIn professional networks.

7. **External repositories validate CEPEJ's influence.** EUR-Lex, OECD, BIICL, and Universidad de los Andes references show CEPEJ AI standards being used as baseline material in EU law/policy, access-to-justice policy, academic events, and public-sector AI guideline catalogues.
