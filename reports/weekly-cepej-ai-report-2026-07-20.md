**Reporting window:** 13 July 2026 00:00–20 July 2026 05:01 UTC

**Prepared:** 20 July 2026

**Scope:** Public references to the CEPEJ AI Charter; CEPEJ and artificial intelligence; the European Commission for the Efficiency of Justice and AI; ethical AI in justice; and AI in judicial systems.

## 1. Executive summary

- Public activity was **low but substantive**. One direct, current-week reference was verified: the European Expertise & Expert Institute (EEEI), an observer member of CEPEJ, updated an analysis on 16 July linking the 46th CEPEJ plenary to human-centred judicial AI and concrete national implementations.
- A second, broader policy signal appeared in the European Commission's 2026 Rule of Law package on 17 July. The Spain chapter discusses court instructions and government projects for judicial AI and cites a 2026 CEPEJ study in its source list. It does **not** cite the CEPEJ AI Charter or generative-AI guidelines directly, so it is treated as an adjacent rather than direct reference.
- No new, date-verifiable LinkedIn post, independent media article, or academic publication directly referring to CEPEJ AI work was found within the strict reporting window. Recent carry-over references from 2 and 10 July remain useful because they show CEPEJ guidance being used in professional and scholarly discussion.
- The clearest theme is not whether courts will use AI, but which functions must remain human. Human oversight, non-substitution of judges, transparency, accountability, training, and controlled deployment continue to dominate references.
- Search coverage included the open web, Council of Europe and European Commission sources, legal and policy sites, DOI/Google Scholar-discoverable material, and publicly indexed LinkedIn content. No legal, academic, social-listening, or LinkedIn database connector was configured for this run.

## 7. Trends and interpretation

1. **The debate is shifting from principles to boundaries in practice.** The EEEI analysis focuses on concrete national deployments and asks what must not be automated. This operational framing is consistent with CEPEJ's earlier Charter and generative-AI guidance, but applies it to live administrative and decision-support projects.
2. **Human authority remains the common denominator.** The direct weekly mention and recent academic/social carry-over all converge on AI as support rather than substitution. Judicial responsibility, effective oversight, and reviewability remain non-negotiable.
3. **Informal adoption is becoming a governance issue.** Recent discussion of “silent AI use” and the EEEI survey's reported awareness gap suggest that institutional procurement rules alone are insufficient; guidance, disclosure norms, mapping, and training are increasingly important.
4. **CEPEJ influence is often indirect.** In high-level policy documents, CEPEJ may appear as a data and evaluation source while AI appears in country-level reform analysis. This is meaningful institutional diffusion, but it should not be mistaken for an explicit citation to the CEPEJ AI Charter.
5. **The weekly sample is too small for a strong directional claim.** Only one direct current-week mention was verified. The themes above are therefore best read as continuity with recent references, not as evidence of a sudden change in public attention.
