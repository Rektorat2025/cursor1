## 1. Executive summary

Direct new public references to CEPEJ AI work during the reporting window were limited. The strongest direct references surfaced in legal and policy repositories that continue to cite the 2018 CEPEJ European Ethical Charter and the December 2025 CEPEJ Guidelines on the use of generative AI for courts as operative benchmarks.

The most important current signal is that CEPEJ's AI standards are moving from soft-law citation into operational governance. Spain's CGPJ Instruction 2/2026, published in the BOE in January and still prominent in current searches, expressly cites both the CEPEJ Charter and CEPEJ(2025)18 Final as reference points for binding judicial AI rules. Policy repositories in Latin America and Europe are indexing CEPEJ's generative AI guidelines as an active public-sector AI instrument.

This week's judicial-AI news was dominated by the Brazilian "prompt injection" incident and follow-on CNJ measures on adversarial security for judicial AI. These Brazilian sources do not generally cite CEPEJ directly, but they strongly validate the risk themes in CEPEJ's current agenda: human oversight, adversarial manipulation, auditability, traceability, security, and training.

CEPEJ's 2026-2027 public agenda continues to be framed around WG-CYBERJUST, the proposed Observatory on AI in Justice, the Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board, judicial data sovereignty, deepfakes/electronic evidence, and an ICT training curriculum for judges.

## 7. Trends and interpretation

- **CEPEJ standards are becoming implementation infrastructure.** The CEPEJ Charter and CEPEJ(2025)18 Final are no longer only ethical references; they are being cited in national judicial AI governance, repository classification, and legal-practice commentary.

- **Adversarial security is now a judicial-governance issue.** The Brazilian prompt-injection case shows that courts need rules for hidden prompts, document sanitization, audit logs, human filters, evidence preservation, and sanctions. This maps directly onto CEPEJ themes of traceability, security, human oversight, and AI literacy.

- **Training is a recurring need.** CEPEJ's planned ICT training curriculum for judges, CNJ's proposed training for magistrates and staff, and LinkedIn/legal commentary on AI literacy all point to the same need: judges and court personnel must understand enough about generative AI to supervise it.

- **The policy focus is shifting from "whether courts may use AI" to "what institutional controls make AI legitimate."** Current references emphasize approved systems, public control, auditability, non-binding AI outputs, judicial independence, and strict limits on using external/public tools with judicial data.

- **Social-media amplification is weaker than legal-policy uptake.** Search-accessible LinkedIn results showed few current direct CEPEJ AI mentions. CEPEJ's influence is more visible in official/legal documents, professional event pages, and public-sector AI repositories than in public social posts.
