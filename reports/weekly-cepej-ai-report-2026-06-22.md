## 1. Executive summary

- Public references this week center on the 46th CEPEJ Plenary Meeting in Sibenik, Croatia, held on 18-19 June 2026 and reported by UIHJ on 21 June 2026. The plenary included direct references to "the judicious use of artificial intelligence", automation in judicial processes, a draft model technology curriculum for judges, a second report on AI use in the judiciary, and a concept note for a judicial AI observatory.
- CEPEJ's AI work is being framed less as abstract ethics and more as operational governance: observatory functions, risk/impact assessment, data sovereignty, public control of infrastructure, explainability, judge training, and safeguards against automation bias.
- Public social discussion remains concentrated among experts and legal academics. Public LinkedIn previews show CEPEJ AI guidance being circulated by Katie Atkinson, Maria Giuliana Civinini, Mireille Hildebrandt, and Alfonso Peralta Gutierrez; full comment threads require LinkedIn sign-in.
- No major general-interest media coverage from the reporting week was found. Professional and policy sources are carrying the signal.

## 7. Trends and interpretation

- The CEPEJ AI conversation is moving from principles to implementation. The 2018 Ethical Charter remains the normative anchor, but public references now emphasize operational tools: assessment questions, procurement safeguards, risk/impact assessment, monitoring, training, observability, and state liability.
- The June 2026 plenary coverage suggests AI and automation are now mainstream agenda items inside CEPEJ rather than side topics. Current workstreams include a judicial AI observatory, a second AI-in-judiciary report, and technology training for judges.
- "Human justice" is the recurring framing. Across CEPEJ documents, LinkedIn expert posts, and legal commentary, the strongest repeated guardrail is that AI may assist but must not replace judicial responsibility, access to a human judge, or reasoned decision-making.
- Data sovereignty and public control are increasingly prominent. CEPEJ's 2026-2027 mandate and generative AI guidelines both point toward secure, customized, public-controlled systems instead of uncontrolled reliance on general-purpose commercial tools.
- Deepfakes and electronic evidence are emerging as the next practical frontier. CEPEJ-GT-CYBERJUST's terms of reference explicitly call out AI-generated deepfakes in the context of electronic evidence.
- Public visibility is expert-heavy. Most accessible social/media references come from legal AI scholars, judges, CEPEJ participants, and professional associations; broad media coverage was not found this week.
