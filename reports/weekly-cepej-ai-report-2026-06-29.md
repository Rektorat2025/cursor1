### 2.4 CEPEJ guidelines on generative AI for courts

- Source: Council of Europe / CEPEJ
- Title: "Guidelines on the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence for Courts"
- Date: Strasbourg, 2025-12-19; adopted at the 45th CEPEJ plenary meeting, 2025-12-04 to 2025-12-05
- Author: CEPEJ Working Group on Cyberjustice and AI (CEPEJ-GT-CYBERJUST), with expert contribution by Javier Ercilla Garcia
- URL: https://rm.coe.int/cepej-2025-18final-en-draft-guidelines-on-the-use-of-generative-ai-for/48802a4ad1
- Why it matters: The central document repeatedly cited in 2026 social, legal, and policy references.
- Quoted snippets:
  - "These Guidelines provide a framework for the implementation of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in the administration of justice, with a focus on ensuring legal certainty, judicial independence with effective human oversight, transparency, and traceability."
  - "These Guidelines are based on the following core principles: the exercise of judicial power is the exclusive responsibility of the courts; effective access to a human judge is always guaranteed; outputs produced by generative AI are never binding; and the use of generative AI in judicial activities and the drafting of decisions must be transparent."
  - "Generative AI should be deployed only when alternative solutions are deemed to be ineffective or inefficient."
  - "Finally, the State should remain liable for any damages arising from the use of generative-AI systems in the administration of justice..."

## 7. Trends and interpretation

- From charter to implementation: Public references increasingly present the 2018 CEPEJ AI Charter as the foundation, but current attention is on implementation instruments: the Assessment Tool, AIAB reports, Resource Centre, generative AI guidelines, and the future Observatory on AI in Justice.
- Human oversight remains the dominant normative anchor: Across CEPEJ documents, EUR-Lex, LinkedIn commentary, and legal analysis, the repeated point is that AI may assist judicial systems but cannot replace judicial decision-making or access to a human judge.
- Generative AI is now the practical stress test: The 2025 CEPEJ guidelines and their social amplification focus on real-world generative AI use in courts: drafting support, summaries, legal research, user-facing information, hallucinations, confidentiality, procurement, infrastructure control, and traceability.
- Monitoring is becoming institutionalized: The 2026-2027 programme and EEEI summary point toward converting the Resource Centre into an Observatory on AI in Justice and linking it to the AIAB, the European Cyberjustice Network, and the Council of Europe AI Framework Convention.
- AI and enforcement are emerging as a secondary topic: UIHJ's plenary report notes future CEPEJ enforcement guideline updates, including AI in enforcement procedures, enforcement on digital assets, and electronic auctions.
- Capacity-building is prominent: The model technology/ICT curriculum for judges appears repeatedly in the June plenary coverage and the 2026-2027 roadmap, suggesting CEPEJ is treating AI literacy as a governance safeguard rather than a peripheral training issue.
- Public discourse is specialized rather than mainstream: Mentions were concentrated in official CEPEJ/Council of Europe materials, professional associations, legal blogs, academic/policy event pages, and LinkedIn posts by legal/AI experts. No mainstream media surge was found.
