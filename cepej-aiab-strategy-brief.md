## Part I — Research Brief: State of AI in European Judicial Systems

### 1. Institutional Context

The **European Commission for the Efficiency of Justice (CEPEJ)** is the Council of Europe body mandated to improve the functioning of judicial systems across 46 member states. Since 2018, it has served as Europe's foremost norm-setting body on AI in the judiciary, producing the continent's first binding ethical standards for judicial AI deployment.

**Key institutional milestones:**

| Year | Milestone |
|------|-----------|
| 2018 | CEPEJ adopts the *European Ethical Charter on the Use of AI in Judicial Systems* — the first European text establishing ethical principles for judicial AI |
| 2019 | CEPEJ establishes the Working Group on Cyberjustice and Artificial Intelligence (CEPEJ-GT-CYBERJUST) |
| 2021 | CEPEJ adopts the *Revised Roadmap for Ensuring an Appropriate Follow-up of the Ethical Charter* |
| 2022 | CEPEJ creates the Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board (AIAB) and the Resource Centre on Cyberjustice and AI |
| 2023 | Resource Centre launched as a publicly accessible repository of AI tools in European justice systems |
| 2024 | Council of Europe adopts the *Framework Convention on AI and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law* (CETS 225), signed in Vilnius — the **first internationally legally binding AI treaty** |
| Dec 2025 | CEPEJ adopts *Guidelines on the Use of Generative AI for Courts* at the 45th Plenary Meeting, Strasbourg |
| Feb 2025 | AIAB publishes the **1st Annual Report on the Use of AI in the Judiciary** |
| 2026–2027 | New CEPEJ mandate launches the **Observatory on AI in Justice** and ICT training curriculum for judges |

---

### 2. Quantitative Landscape: Key Statistics

#### 2.1 AI Tool Adoption in European Courts

- **125 cyberjustice and AI tools** have been identified and catalogued in the CEPEJ Resource Centre on Cyberjustice and AI as of end-2024 — sourced primarily from European jurisdictions.
- **46%** of respondents in CEPEJ's European Cyberjustice Network (ECN) survey (October 2025) confirmed active use of **generative AI in courts**.
  - Of those, **74%** rely on standard off-the-shelf applications (Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT, DeepL).
  - **52%** use customised or tailored applications.
  - The dominant judicial use case is **document summarisation and editorial support**.
  - **Zero** respondents reported using generative AI for quantitative estimation of legal outcomes.

#### 2.2 Legal Professional Sector (Future Ready Lawyer 2024 Report — 712 respondents, US + 9 European countries)

- **68%** of law firms and **76%** of corporate legal departments use generative AI **at least weekly**.
- **35%** of corporate legal departments and **33%** of law firms use AI **daily**.
- Primary use cases: document automation (67%), time management (66%), strategic planning (62%).
- **The Netherlands** leads European daily generative AI adoption at **42%**; Germany follows at **38%**.
- **58%** of law firms plan to increase AI investment over the next three years; **73%** of corporate legal departments plan the same.

#### 2.3 Regulatory Compliance Deadlines

- **2 February 2025:** EU AI Act obligations on AI literacy and prohibited practices entered force.
- **2 August 2025:** EU AI Act governance rules and obligations for general-purpose AI models became applicable.
- **2 August 2026:** Full compliance required for **high-risk AI systems** — a category that explicitly includes AI used by judicial authorities for **legal research, interpretation, and decision-support**.

### 3. The Regulatory and Ethical Framework

#### 3.1 The CEPEJ Ethical Charter (2018) — Five Core Principles

The *European Ethical Charter on the Use of AI in Judicial Systems* remains the foundational normative document. Its five principles are:

1. **Respect for fundamental rights** — AI design and implementation must comply with the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) and Convention 108+.
2. **Non-discrimination** — Prevention of any development or intensification of discrimination against individuals or groups.
3. **Quality and security** — Use of certified, secure sources and models conceived in a multidisciplinary manner.
4. **Transparency, impartiality and fairness** — Data processing methods must be accessible and understandable; external audits must be authorised.
5. **User control** — Citizens and judicial professionals must retain meaningful oversight and the ability to challenge AI-generated outputs.

#### 3.2 CETS 225 — Council of Europe AI Framework Convention (2024)

- Adopted 17 May 2024 by the Council of Europe Committee of Ministers; opened for signature in Vilnius, 5 September 2024.
- The **first internationally legally binding treaty** ensuring AI systems are consistent with human rights, democracy, and the rule of law.
- **Article 5** mandates that parties adopt measures to prevent AI systems from undermining the integrity, independence and effectiveness of democratic institutions — explicitly including **judicial independence** and **access to justice**.
- Each party must establish **at least one independent oversight mechanism** to monitor compliance.
- Countries eligible to join include all 46 Council of Europe member states as well as non-member states and the European Union.

#### 3.3 CEPEJ Guidelines on Generative AI for Courts (December 2025)

Adopted at the 45th Plenary Meeting (Strasbourg, 4–5 December 2025), these guidelines establish a binding operational framework for generative AI deployment in courts, with four non-negotiable principles:

1. The exercise of **judicial power is the exclusive responsibility of courts** — AI cannot substitute judicial reasoning.
2. **Effective access to a human judge** is always guaranteed.
3. Outputs produced by generative AI are **never binding**.
4. The use of generative AI in judicial activities must be **transparent and traceable**.

Additional requirements include: pseudonymisation of data, exclusive public-authority control of data and infrastructure, audit rights over algorithms and training data, and portability guarantees allowing courts to change AI providers without loss of functionality.

#### 3.4 EU AI Act (Regulation EU 2024/1689)

- Classifies AI systems used by **judicial authorities for legal research and interpretation** as **high-risk** under Annex III.
- Requirements for high-risk judicial AI systems include: mandatory risk management systems, data governance standards, detailed technical documentation, traceability logs, clear instructions for deployers, human oversight mechanisms, and robustness/cybersecurity standards.
- Public bodies deploying these systems must conduct a **Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA)** under Article 27 before deployment.
- Full compliance mandatory from **2 August 2026**.

### 4. Current Use Cases and Tool Typology

The CEPEJ Resource Centre categorises identified tools across the following functional areas:

| Category | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Document search & discovery** | Searchable collections of case-law, legal text analysis | Germany: OberLandesGerichts-Assistant for classifying claims in mass litigation |
| **Triaging, allocation & workflow automation** | Facilitating case management and routing | French SelExpert digital expert-matching platform |
| **Summarisation & drafting assistance** | Editorial support, text generation, correction | Microsoft Copilot, DeepL, tailored LLMs |
| **Citizen-facing information tools** | Guiding citizens through justice processes | Portugal: PGJ (Practical Guide to Justice) — machine-learning-based conversational assistant |
| **Anonymisation** | Automated redaction of personally identifiable information | Spanish neural learning-based anonymisation tool |
| **Speech-to-text & hearing transcription** | Automated recording, transcription, speaker identification | Spain: "Textualisation" court hearing tool |
| **Online Dispute Resolution (ODR)** | Digital platforms for resolving disputes without physical hearings | Various national platforms |

> **No tool in the Resource Centre is used for autonomous judicial decision-making.** All identified systems function as support tools, requiring human judicial oversight.

### 5. Expert Voices and Practitioner Sentiment

#### 5.1 Judicial Officeholders

> *"The use of AI by the judiciary must be consistent with its overarching obligation to protect the integrity of the administration of justice and uphold the rule of law. I welcome the publication of the latest AI Guidance, which reinforces this principle and the personal responsibility judicial office holders have for all material produced in their name."*
>
> — **Lord Justice Birss**, Lead Judge for Artificial Intelligence, Courts and Tribunals Judiciary (UK), October 2025

#### 5.2 Academic and Expert Analysis

> *"Artificial intelligence is perceived as a friend when it reduces the administrative burden, facilitates the rapid identification of case law, or provides a statistical 'second eye' on similar cases. At the same time, AI becomes an adversary when it appears to standardise the solution, introduces risks of opaque bias, or undermines the personal responsibility of the judge in the eyes of the public."*
>
> — **LexEthica Research (2025)**, *AI, Friend or Adversary of the Judge?*

> *"There is a strategic urgency to train magistrates correctly and continuously. Judges do not need to become systems engineers, but they do need a minimum vocabulary and a basic understanding of how models are trained, how structural bias propagates, and what the difference is between a deterministic and a probabilistic system. Without these benchmarks, there is a risk that legally correct solutions will be built on erroneous technical premises."*
>
> — **LexEthica Research (2025)**

> *"Court decisions produced by AI tech tools could potentially threaten the basic principles of human rights and the rule of law as there are severe concerns over the protection and right to privacy, algorithmic discrimination and bias, and reduced public trust on AI justice."*
>
> — **Public Policy Institute (Ireland, 2024)**, *Judging by AI: Compromised Independence and a Threat to Human Rights?*

#### 5.3 Identified Risks in Expert Literature

- **Algorithmic bias:** AI systems trained on historical datasets may replicate and amplify existing societal and judicial biases, which is particularly dangerous in human rights contexts where non-discrimination is a core principle.
- **Hallucinations:** AI-generated legal references, citations, or case law may be fabricated, with serious consequences for proceedings.
- **Opacity:** Black-box models make it impossible to verify how a result was derived, undermining the principle of reasoned judicial decision-making.
- **Over-reliance:** Structural pressure on judges facing heavy caseloads may incentivise uncritical acceptance of AI outputs.
- **Deepfakes in evidence:** AI-generated synthetic media poses growing challenges for the integrity of electronic evidence in proceedings.
- **Data sovereignty:** Reliance on commercial general-purpose LLMs from major technology companies creates risks of data leakage, external interference, and loss of institutional control.

## Part II — Strategy Brief for CEPEJ AIAB

# Advancing Human-Centred Artificial Intelligence in European Justice Systems

## A Persuasive Strategy Brief for the CEPEJ Artificial Intelligence Advisory Board (AIAB)

**Addressed to:** Members of the CEPEJ AIAB
**Period:** 2026–2028 Strategic Horizon
**Objective:** To position the AIAB as the primary authoritative body guiding the responsible, rights-protective integration of AI across the 46-member state European judicial space — and to turn the current regulatory convergence moment into a durable institutional advantage.

---

### Executive Summary

Europe's courts are crossing a threshold. The deployment of AI tools in judicial systems — once the province of pilot projects and experimental platforms — is now mainstream, driven by the irreversible commercial availability of generative AI, mounting caseload pressure, and the ambitions of digital justice reform programmes. **46% of Council of Europe member states already report generative AI use in courts.** **125 tools are catalogued and operational.** A legally binding international treaty (CETS 225) and a comprehensive risk regulation (EU AI Act) are either in force or approaching full applicability.

The CEPEJ AIAB stands at the intersection of all of these forces. It is the only European expert body with the mandate, the credibility, and the normative infrastructure to translate principles into practice across jurisdictional lines. The question before the Board is not whether AI will transform European justice — it already is. The question is whether that transformation will be shaped by public institutions accountable to democratic values, or by commercial forces optimising for efficiency at the expense of rights.

**This brief argues for three strategic imperatives:**

1. **Consolidate authority** — transform the AIAB from an advisory function into the analytical engine of the new Observatory on AI in Justice.
2. **Operationalise the normative framework** — close the gap between ethics-on-paper and ethics-in-deployment through assessment tools, certification guidance, and auditable compliance standards.
3. **Build judicial capacity at scale** — launch a pan-European AI literacy programme for judges that makes human oversight meaningful, not ceremonial.

---

### Strategic Imperative 1: Consolidate Authority — From Advisory Board to Observatory Engine

#### The Opportunity

The 2026–2027 CEPEJ mandate explicitly calls for the transformation of the Resource Centre on Cyberjustice and AI into a **fully-fledged Observatory on AI in Justice**. This is a structural opportunity that will not repeat. The Observatory is designed to support implementation of CETS 225 in the judicial field — making the AIAB the de facto technical authority for the only internationally binding AI treaty in the world.

#### The Problem the Board Must Solve

The current Resource Centre lists 125 tools. But **listing is not governing**. Member states are deploying AI systems of wildly varying quality, transparency, and rights-compliance. Many tools operate inside internal court IT systems with no public accountability. Commercial off-the-shelf tools — used by **74% of courts that deploy generative AI** — come with terms of service written by technology companies, not judicial authorities. There is no systematic process for evaluating whether any of these tools meet the CEPEJ Ethical Charter's five principles, let alone the binding requirements of the EU AI Act.

#### The Strategic Recommendation

The AIAB must drive the Observatory to do four things that the Resource Centre cannot:

**1. Mandatory Structured Assessment, Not Voluntary Listing**
Move from a voluntary submission model to a structured notification framework. Member states should be expected — and supported — to complete the CEPEJ Ethical Charter Assessment Tool as a condition of formal recognition of any judicial AI system. The AIAB should publish standardised assessment templates and an annual compliance dashboard.

**2. Proactive Risk-Tiering**
Apply a risk-tiering methodology to the 125+ catalogued tools, distinguishing between low-risk productivity tools (document summarisation, translation) and high-risk decision-support or prediction tools. This tiering should directly map onto EU AI Act Annex III classifications and CETS 225 obligations, providing member states with a clear compliance pathway.

**3. Annual Intelligence Reporting**
The 1st AIAB Annual Report (February 2025) established a valuable precedent. The 2nd report — due in the 2026–2027 mandate — must go further: it should include country-level analysis, risk-tiering findings, compliance gap identification, and — critically — examples of both good practice and concerning deployments. Naming both excellence and risk is what separates a credible observatory from a catalogue.

**4. Deepfake and Synthetic Evidence Early Warning Function**
The integrity of electronic evidence is under direct threat from AI-generated synthetic media. The Observatory should establish an **early-warning sub-function** specifically tracking the emergence of deepfake-related evidentiary challenges in European courts. This is a concrete new contribution the AIAB can make that no other Council of Europe body is positioned to deliver.

#### Why This Argument Is Compelling

The window for AIAB to claim this role is narrow. If the Observatory is built without a strong AIAB mandate at its core, the technical authority will default to the CEPEJ Secretariat or — worse — to ad hoc working group reports. The Board must make the case, through its 2nd Annual Report and through proactive engagement with the WG-CYBERJUST mandate, that **expert independent technical assessment is not optional infrastructure — it is the Observatory's primary deliverable**.

### Strategic Imperative 2: Operationalise the Normative Framework — Closing the Compliance Gap

#### The Problem

The CEPEJ's normative achievements are substantial: the 2018 Ethical Charter, the 2023 Assessment Tool, the 2024 CETS 225, the 2025 Generative AI Guidelines. But normative depth without operational specificity produces parchment compliance — courts that can truthfully claim adherence to principles without meaningfully implementing them.

Consider the core tension: **76% of corporate legal departments use generative AI weekly**, the commercial market is moving at technology-company speed, and the average European court system moves at a pace governed by public procurement cycles and political consensus. The risk is that by the time a court formally adopts AI, it has been using it informally for years, without any compliance review.

Additionally, **2 August 2026** is a compliance cliff for member states: all high-risk AI systems in judicial use must comply with EU AI Act requirements. Many courts are not prepared. The AIAB has an immediate opportunity to serve as the bridge between the EU regulatory framework and the Council of Europe human-rights framework — two overlapping but distinct regimes — in a way that neither the European Commission nor the Council of Europe Secretariat can do alone.

#### The Strategic Recommendations

**1. Publish a Dual-Framework Compliance Guide for Courts**
The AIAB should produce a practical, plain-language compliance guide that maps the CEPEJ Ethical Charter's five principles against the EU AI Act's high-risk system requirements and CETS 225 Article 5 obligations. Courts should be able to use a single document to assess compliance across all three frameworks simultaneously. This is a gap the market has not filled and that the Board is uniquely positioned to fill.

**2. Operationalise Data Sovereignty Requirements**
The December 2025 Generative AI Guidelines make clear that **judicial data must remain under the exclusive control of judicial authorities**, that courts must retain audit rights over algorithms and training data, and that courts must be able to change AI providers without loss of functionality. These are sound principles — but they are operationally demanding for courts with limited IT capacity.

The AIAB should publish a **Technical Implementation Guidance Note** on data sovereignty, providing:
- Minimum contractual requirements for commercial AI vendors supplying judicial systems.
- A model procurement checklist for courts adopting AI tools.
- Guidance on local LLM deployment and open-source alternatives where data sovereignty is paramount.
- A framework for auditing AI system compliance post-deployment.

**3. Establish a Living Assessment Tool Review Cycle**
The CEPEJ Ethical Charter Assessment Tool (2023) was designed to be evolutionary. The AIAB should formalise an **annual review cycle** to update it against new technology developments (generative AI, multimodal systems, agentic AI) and new regulatory requirements. The first update should address the specific characteristics of LLM-based systems that were not contemplated in the original tool.

**4. Address the Hallucination and Fabrication Risk Directly**
AI hallucinations — the generation of incorrect or fabricated legal references, case citations, and facts — are already producing documented problems in courts worldwide. The AIAB should issue a **specific advisory note** on hallucination risk in judicial contexts, including:
- A taxonomy of hallucination types and their legal consequences.
- Verification requirements (checking AI outputs against authoritative primary sources).
- Disclosure obligations when AI-assisted drafting is used in judicial documents.
- Training requirements for judicial staff using generative AI tools.

### Strategic Imperative 3: Build Judicial Capacity at Scale — Making Human Oversight Meaningful

#### The Problem

Every normative framework produced by CEPEJ, every guideline, every principle, and every regulatory requirement converges on a single point: **effective human oversight by a competent judge**. Judicial independence, the exclusivity of judicial power, the non-binding nature of AI outputs — all of these protections are hollow unless the judge exercising oversight actually understands what they are overseeing.

The evidence is stark. As LexEthica's 2025 research documents, judges today navigate AI both as a tool in their daily work and as a central issue in litigation — civil liability, data protection, criminal evidence, labour disputes — without "established conceptual frameworks." They need not become software engineers, but they must understand how models are trained, how structural bias propagates, and what the difference is between a deterministic and a probabilistic system.

The ICT Training Curriculum for Judges, due to be presented at the June 2026 CEPEJ Plenary, represents the most important capacity-building initiative the Council of Europe has ever attempted in the digital justice space. Its design will determine whether human oversight is a meaningful safeguard or a legal fiction.

#### The Strategic Recommendations

**1. Design the Training Curriculum Around Judicial Decision Points, Not Technical Architecture**
The risk in designing AI literacy training for judges is over-indexing on technical explanation. Judges do not need to understand transformers or attention mechanisms. They need to understand AI behaviour at the **moment it enters their professional judgment**. The curriculum should be structured around decision points:

- *When evaluating an AI-assisted document:* How to identify potential bias, hallucination, and data quality issues.
- *When AI evidence is presented by parties:* How to assess the reliability, provenance, and limitations of AI-generated analysis.
- *When courts deploy AI tools internally:* What questions to ask vendors, what audit rights to insist upon, and what transparency to require.
- *When AI-generated deepfakes are alleged in electronic evidence:* What verification standards apply, and where to refer for technical assistance.

**2. Build in Differentiated Pathways by Judicial Role**
A first-instance civil judge in a small jurisdiction faces different AI challenges than a commercial court judge in a large urban court system. The curriculum should offer a **common foundational module** (fundamental rights and AI, key concepts, oversight obligations) and **role-differentiated advanced modules** (evidence evaluation, data protection litigation, criminal evidence, etc.).

**3. Partner with the European Cyberjustice Network for Delivery**
The European Cyberjustice Network (ECN) exists precisely to facilitate peer learning and practical knowledge exchange across European judiciaries. The AIAB should recommend that the ECN serve as the primary delivery vehicle for the training curriculum, ensuring that peer-to-peer practitioner learning — which is how judicial cultures actually change — is central to the programme design, not supplementary to it.

**4. Develop a Technology Glossary Update Cadence**
The CEPEJ Technology Glossary is valuable but risks obsolescence. The AIAB should formalise a **semi-annual update cycle**, with updates published on the Observatory platform, to ensure that judicial professionals consulting the glossary are working from a current vocabulary. Terms like "large language model," "agentic AI," "retrieval-augmented generation," and "synthetic media" should be added and defined in plain-language judicial context.

### Summary: Three Imperatives, One Coherent Strategy

The AIAB's strategic position in 2026–2028 is exceptional — but only if the Board seizes it. The three imperatives outlined in this brief are mutually reinforcing:

- **Consolidating authority** through the Observatory gives the Board the platform and institutional legitimacy to govern.
- **Operationalising the normative framework** gives member states the practical tools to comply — and gives the Board the enforcement credibility to require compliance.
- **Building judicial capacity** ensures that every rights safeguard the Board advocates for is actually exercised by a competent human judge in a real courtroom.

Together, these three imperatives represent a coherent transition: from **AIAB as advisory board** to **AIAB as the authoritative institutional conscience of AI in European justice**.

The stakes are not abstract. They are the right to a fair trial, the independence of the judiciary, the integrity of evidence, and public trust in the rule of law — all of which are being reshaped by technology, right now, in courts across 46 member states. The AIAB exists to ensure that reshaping serves human rights and democratic legitimacy.

**The Board should act accordingly.**
